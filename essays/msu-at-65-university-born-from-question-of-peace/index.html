MSU at 65: The University Born from a Question of Peace

MSU was born from a much larger question: How could peace and genuine inclusion be achieved in Mindanao, where many Moro and Indigenous communities had long remained politically, economically, and educationally marginalized?

Written by Raihan A. Yusoph1 September 2026 · 5 min read

On September 1, 2026, Mindanao State University marks its 65th anniversary. For many of us, MSU is a familiar university, the home of generations of students, teachers, scholars, professionals, public servants, and leaders. Its main campus has become inseparable from the history of Marawi City and from the intellectual life of Mindanao. Yet behind the familiar name is a history that many MSUans themselves may not know.

MSU was born from a much larger question: How could peace and genuine inclusion be achieved in Mindanao, where many Moro and Indigenous communities had long remained politically, economically, and educationally marginalized?

In the years following Philippine independence, most major institutions of higher education remained concentrated in Manila and other predominantly Christian urban centers. Mindanao, despite its size, population, and importance, remained distant from the centers of national power. For many young Moros, obtaining a university education often meant leaving Mindanao altogether. Senator Ahmad Domocao Alonto believed that this arrangement reproduced inequality. Sending a few deserving students to Manila through scholarships was important, but it could never substitute for building a major institution of higher learning inside Mindanao itself.

This idea was also shaped by the troubled politics of the 1950s. One largely forgotten catalyst in MSU's history was the Hadji Kamlon uprising in Sulu. The uprising became one of the most serious armed challenges faced by the Philippine government in the postwar period. It demonstrated that recurring unrest among the Moros could not simply be solved through military campaigns. Within this atmosphere, education increasingly came to be viewed as part of a broader effort to address exclusion, build relationships between communities, and integrate Mindanao more meaningfully into national life.

Here is one interesting piece of MSU: the university was initially considered for Sulu.

Because the Kamlon uprising had brought Sulu to national attention, the province was proposed as a possible location for the new university. Difficulties in securing sufficient land, however, eventually led the proposed institution to be relocated to Dansalan City, now Marawi City. What might have been a university in Sulu consequently became an institution overlooking Lake Lanao.

Its establishment was far from easy.

Senator Domocao, who championed the proposal in Congress, encountered skepticism and even prejudice. According to his son, Dr. Ahmad Domocao Alonto Jr., Congressman Ismael Veloso of Davao questioned why such a university should be located in Dansalan, arguing that the people there would not know how to use it and that government money might simply be wasted.

Senator Domocao reportedly answered the criticism with remarkable simplicity. If his people were being described as uneducated, then that was precisely why they needed a university.

There is something powerful in that response. The argument against establishing a university in the Moro heartland became the very argument for creating one.

Senator Domocao understood educational disadvantage as the consequence of historical neglect, rather than proof that people were incapable of higher learning. For him, Muslims, Christians, and Indigenous peoples should have the opportunity to study together and participate equally in public life. MSU was therefore imagined as something more ambitious than another government school. It was envisioned as a shared intellectual space in a region deeply divided by history, religion, ethnicity, migration, and conflict.

Another little-known detail is that the institution was originally legislated as the University of Mindanao. Senator Domocao authored Republic Act No. 1387, which formally provided for its establishment in Dansalan City. The institution eventually took shape on September 1, 1961, the date from which MSU now counts its anniversaries. Its mandate was subsequently strengthened, and Republic Act No. 3791, enacted in 1963, authored by Congressman Haroun Al-Rashid Lucman, formally adopted the name Mindanao State University.

Thus, the story behind the name “Mindanao State University” is itself part of its history.

But perhaps the most important thing to remember on MSU's 65th anniversary is why the university was placed in Marawi in the first place.

It was a declaration that knowledge did not have to come only from Manila. It challenged the idea that the Moro homeland was merely a troublesome frontier to be administered from elsewhere. Establishing a major state university in Marawi affirmed that Mindanao could itself become a center of scholarship, professional training, political discussion, cultural expression, and knowledge production. Senator Domocao's persistence eventually earned him recognition as the “Brainchild of the Mindanao State University.”

Over the succeeding decades, that small experiment grew into a university system stretching across Mindanao, Sulu, and Palawan. Its classrooms brought together students whose communities had sometimes encountered one another through histories of conflict and distrust. Muslims, Christians, and Indigenous peoples studied, debated, formed friendships, disagreed, organized, and imagined different futures within the same institution. This helps explain why MSU eventually acquired one of its most memorable descriptions: the “Melting Pot of the South.”

Sixty-five years later, perhaps this is the history worth remembering.

MSU was created because Mindanao needed more than peace enforced by arms. It needed an opportunity. It needed education. It needed a place where people historically separated by religion, ethnicity, geography, and political experience could encounter one another as students and as equals.

The university was born from the problems of its time, but also from an extraordinary confidence in what education could accomplish.

So, as we celebrate the 65th anniversary of Mindanao State University, we celebrate more than the founding of a school on the hills of Marawi. We remember an idea that the people of Mindanao deserved to produce knowledge in their own homeland; that education could restore dignity to communities long pushed to the margins; and that a university could become a meeting place where Muslims, Christians, and Indigenous peoples might participate in building a shared future.

Happy 65th Anniversary, Mindanao State University.

May we never forget that MSU was created not merely to educate minds, but to help answer one of the most difficult questions in our history: how can the diverse people of Mindanao live, learn, and build a future together with dignity?
